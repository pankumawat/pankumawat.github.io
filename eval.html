Quick read / Page 1 of 3 · The big idea

Do not confuse a convincing answer with a trustworthy one.

An AI evaluation, or eval, checks an AI-generated output against the user's request, reliable evidence, and explicit quality criteria. Think of it as marking an exam: the answer needs more than fluent writing to earn a good grade.

For Insights, the output is an interactive report. It may look polished while using the wrong total, answering the wrong question, or showing filters that do nothing. An eval makes these problems observable and repeatable instead of relying on a reviewer's first impression.

Four questions to ask

Is it true? Numbers and claims agree with the source.
Is it relevant? It serves this audience and decision.
Is it clear? The reader understands the finding and its significance.
Does it work? Charts, filters, and drilldowns behave correctly.

Four terms to remember

Rubric: a criterion with defined scoring anchors.
Judge: code, a browser test, a person, or an AI assessor.
Oracle: an independently computed or reviewed expected answer.
Gate: a condition that must pass, regardless of the average score.

Example: a revenue-exposure report can contain individually correct clause totals but overstate portfolio exposure by counting the same contract twice. Good prose or a high visual score cannot fix that arithmetic error.

The core rule: compare the final output to independent evidence. Do not ask the generator whether its own answer is correct and treat that explanation as proof. Use code for exact facts, browser tests for behavior, and scoped human or AI judgment for meaning.

Quick read / Page 2 of 3 · The Avalon reference

Avalon checks quality in layers, not with one universal judge.

Verified Avalon Avalon generates HTML artifacts and evaluates them with complementary tools. A structural check can detect broken HTML; it cannot tell whether a report supports a CFO's decision. A visual judge can assess hierarchy; it cannot reliably prove a financial aggregation.

Structure and behavior

L1–L2: validate syntax, document structure, conventions, and Avalon-specific protocols.

L3: load the artifact in a real browser and test rendering, navigation, focus, and selected interactions.

Content and presentation

L3.5: judge 17 content dimensions, including accuracy, completeness, coherence, and usefulness.

L4: judge 11 visual dimensions from captured screenshots and selected UI states.

Specialist checks add report quality (L8), dashboard quality (L9), source accuracy and coverage (L10), content fit (L11), and opt-in chart quality (L12). Not every execution path runs every layer.

How a judge works: load a dimension-specific prompt, attach relevant source and artifact evidence, request an anchored 1–5 rating, and retain the reasoning and evidence. The accuracy_and_precision prompt explicitly permits valid paraphrases and mathematical derivations; it tells the judge to search the whole source before alleging fabrication.

How comparisons work: Avalon can grade one output, compare two outputs with presentation order swapped, or aggregate several judges. These help assess quality, but agreement between models is not proof of truth.

Borrow the architecture, not every rule. Avalon has artifact-specific requirements, bounded evidence capture, path-dependent overall status, and advisory quality checks. Its chart checks do not independently recompute all derived arithmetic. For Insights, add a structured-data oracle and explicit trust gates rather than treating an Avalon "pass" as a certificate.

01 / Start here

An eval is a structured way to ask, "Was this good?"

An evaluation, usually shortened to eval, is a repeatable assessment of an AI system's output against explicit expectations and evidence. It replaces "looks impressive" with "here is what passed, what failed, and why."

The everyday analogy

A student submits an essay. A teacher uses the question, source material, marking criteria, and the essay itself to assess it. A beautiful cover cannot compensate for made-up facts.

For AI, the request is the question, the final report is the essay, the dataset is the evidence, and a rubric is the marking guide.

The Insights version

A user asks for renewal risk or revenue exposure for a named audience. Insights generates a report. A separate evaluator asks:

Are the numbers right? Does it answer this person's question? Do the charts and filters actually work? Can the reader make a decision?

Evaluation = test case + final output + evidence + explicit checks + recorded result
Test case
A particular request, attached to a known dataset and evaluation conditions.
Rubric
A definition of quality, with concrete scoring anchors. "Audience fit" is a dimension; descriptions of scores 1–5 make it a usable rubric.
Judge
The assessor: deterministic code, a browser test, a human, or another AI model. Different judges are suited to different questions.
Oracle
An independently established expected result. For example, a small program recomputes an exposure total directly from frozen input records.
Ground truth
Reviewed reference facts or labels. It need not be one ideal report. Several different reports can be correct.
Benchmark
A versioned collection of cases used to compare systems or releases consistently.
Regression
A new version does worse on a previously working case. A repeatable evaluation helps reveal it.
Gate
A condition that must pass before accepting a result. A high average score must not cancel a confirmed invented figure.

Why not just read a few reports?

AI can produce a polished, plausible report that invents a number, mixes currencies, ignores a requested cohort, or gives a CFO a contract-review worklist instead of an executive decision summary. It can also vary between runs. A repeatable eval catches known failure modes and makes improvements measurable.

Correct is not useful

Every number may be accurate, but the report may answer the wrong question.

Useful-looking is not correct

A compelling recommendation may rest on an unsupported calculation.

A screenshot is not behavior

A filter can look perfect yet leave the chart and document list unchanged.

An eval is not training, and a judge is not an oracle. Running a judge does not automatically improve the generator. An AI's confident explanation is not proof; it needs checkable evidence. Evaluation produces findings that people use to change prompts, code, data handling, or product requirements.

02 / Define what is under test

One request in. One final report out. Everything between is a black box.

The supplied Insights architecture explorer explains how the product goes from collection data to an interactive report. For this evaluation, the internal generation process is deliberately opaque. No approval step, internal plan, intermediate prompt, or implementation trace is required to evaluate the final result.

Five request fields are not enough to prove a number. They tell us what the report should do. To determine whether "$150,000 exposed" is correct, the evaluator separately needs the exact extracted records supplied to that run. The two workbooks provide scenario requests, not the underlying contract dataset or generated reports.

What may enter the scenario

Persona, Goal, Report Title (paste), Audience (paste), and User Intent. Their exact supplied contents are shown in Chapter 7 below.

Persona and Goal can frame the test case. Forward them only if the real generation interface supports them; do not silently invent API fields or combine text differently between releases.

What belongs to the evaluator

A frozen collection snapshot, agreed metric definitions, reference calculations, a pinned "today" for time-dependent tests, final artifact captures, and scoring policies.

These are test fixtures and evaluation metadata. They are not additional spreadsheet columns or hidden instructions sent to the generator.

What this guide intentionally does not evaluate

Internal reasoning, hidden plans, approval UX, or intermediate transformations. Product sharing, editing, export, and refresh need their own tests; they are not prerequisites for this initial-input-to-final-report evaluation. Export is also marked out of scope for V1 in PRD section 5.4, despite a conflicting summary later in that document.

03 / A separate evaluation pipeline

The evaluator needs evidence that does not come from the answer it is grading.

Proposed Keep the generation pipeline and evaluation pipeline separate. In particular, never accept a generated KPI table as the truth used to validate that same KPI table.

Use code for arithmetic

Was the sum $150k? Did 3 of 10 records fail a check? Does the selected cohort contain exactly these IDs?

Use a browser for behavior

Does choosing USD update all relevant charts? Does clicking a bar show the contracts behind that bar?

Use judgment for meaning

Is this useful to a CFO? Are recommendations warranted? Does the report explain why the result matters?

What happens during a judge call?

1

Load a dimension-specific prompt

For example, prompts/l35/accuracy_and_precision.txt. It specifies the judge's role, the exact dimension, exclusions, score anchors, and response format.

2

Attach the relevant evidence

Artifact text, the original request when needed, source text for grounding, or screenshots for visual assessment. A missing source can make a grounding check inapplicable; it does not make the artifact accurate.

3

Request a structured assessment

Text rubrics return a score, reasoning, and evidence quotes. Visual rubrics use evidence observations. The orchestration stores results and computes severity-weighted layer summaries.

4

Inspect the evidence, not just the number

A judge can miss a passage, misread a chart, or invent an explanation. The strongest checks verify the judge's claimed evidence against the actual source and artifact.

The full L3.5 and L4 dimension lists

Content (17): accuracy_and_precision, completeness, content_hierarchy, data_and_evidence, depth_and_substance, examples_and_illustrations, fit_for_purpose, information_grouping, logical_flow, overall_coherence, overall_usefulness, prompt_adherence, relevance, sentence_clarity, technical_accuracy, tone_fit, writing_mechanics.

Visual (11): clickable_cohesion, color_balance, contrast, design_system_consistency, graphic_integration, information_hierarchy, section_separation, text_readability, visual_fit_for_purpose, visual_grouping, visual_overall_usefulness.

Three ways Avalon compares outputs

Pointwise: grade one artifact against fixed rubrics. This provides an absolute diagnosis, subject to the quality of the judge and rubric.

Pairwise: compare A and B on the same request and source. Avalon judges both presentation orders, inverts the second result, then averages. The preference scale is −2 to +2; disagreement after swapping positions is recorded. This reduces order bias but does not prove either answer is true.

Multi-judge consensus: aggregate per-check assessments from active judges and retain their individual reasoning. A configured panel is not necessarily the actual panel if calls fail or checks skip. Agreement can still reflect a shared blind spot.

Automation, models, and why "Avalon has evals" does not mean every request runs them

The AutoEval tools can generate artifacts over a bundled 30-document Markdown corpus, evaluate them, and publish inspection artifacts and run summaries. The release script uses a fixed layer set including L3, L3.5, L4, L8 and L10; not every available layer runs on every release report.

The checkout's general judge default is Claude Sonnet 4.6 via the configured gateway. A configured consensus panel uses Claude, GPT-5.4-mini, and Gemini 2.5 Pro. Some visual paths have a different default; release automation disables a visual-model swap to keep comparisons consistent. Pin the actual models in a run manifest rather than relying on these defaults.

A separate, older eval_harness defaults to a mock registry with synthetic scores; its real registry is an explicit choice and still has stubbed layers. Mock results are not evidence of artifact quality. CI evaluation is conditional, not universal.

The primary production creation entrypoint skips the optional verifier for latency. The optional verifier is not the same thing as running the full offline evaluation stack, and it does not imply a general automatic repair loop.

What to borrow: separate checks by failure mode, use anchored judge prompts, collect browser evidence, preserve per-check reasoning, and compare on the same source. What not to copy blindly: Avalon-specific HTML protocols, raw-source coverage expectations, count-based dashboard incentives, or its advisory quality gates.

05 / Read the score correctly

A score is a summary. A gate is a decision. Coverage tells you how much was checked.

Verified Avalon

Severity-weighted scores

Avalon's severity weights are critical 1.0, high 0.6, medium 0.3, low 0.1.

Deterministic layer score = weighted passes / applicable weight. Judge layer score = weighted mean of rating / 5. A native rubric rating of at least 3 is a passing check.

Proposed Insights policy

Do not average away a trust failure

Keep exact correctness gates separate from qualitative scores. A confirmed invented figure or an empty required report section fails the PRD bar even if the design and prose receive excellent ratings.

Unknown and evaluator error must remain visible. Neither is evidence of a pass.

Deterministic example: critical pass (1.0), high fail (0.6), medium pass (0.3)
Layer score = (1.0 + 0.3) / (1.0 + 0.6 + 0.3) = 0.684

In Avalon's deterministic aggregation, any critical failure, at least two high failures, or a score below 0.60 produces a layer failure. One high failure or a score below 0.80 produces a warning. Quality layers have different thresholds: a critical rating of 2 or less or a layer score below 0.50 fails the layer; a critical 3, high 2 or less, or score below 0.70 warns.

Overall status is path-dependent in Avalon. The pointwise overall status uses L1–L3 as hard checks and selected quality layers as advisory; it omits L8/L9/L10. The consensus path considers more quality layers, still advisory. L12 remains separate. An overall "pass" is therefore not a certificate that every grounding rubric passed.

Three scales that are easy to confuse

ValueWhat it meansExample
Native rubric scoreThe judge's anchored rating, 1–5.A judge returns 4.
Normalized layer contributionThe native rating divided by 5 before aggregation.4 / 5 = 0.80.
Uniform dashboard displayA 0–1 value remapped as 1 + 4 × value.0.80 displays as 4.20, not the native 4.

Why one big number can be dangerous

Illustration
4.75 / 5

Fifteen dimensions score 5; grounding scores 1 because the report contains a confirmed invented figure. The unweighted average is still 4.75.

PRD trust bar
Not acceptable

The PRD's fixed quality bar is zero invented figures and no empty sections. A near-perfect average does not satisfy it. There are no PRD numeric targets for the other dimensions yet.

Always report applicability and coverage

Proposed Store pass, fail, not_applicable, unknown, and error separately. State how many eligible claims, charts, and interaction states were verified. A missing dataset makes factual correctness unverified; it must not produce a perfect score.

Avalon's L12 already illustrates the issue: its data-fidelity ratio excludes unknown marks from the verified-versus-unsupported denominator while exposing coverage separately. "All checked marks were right" is weaker than "all marks were checked and right." Older summary paths can also count some skipped checks as passes; do not copy that convention into a trust gate.

08 / Data correctness is more than "the judge liked it"

Verify the path from records to numbers to claims.

Proposed For Insights, the strongest adaptation to Avalon is a structured-data oracle: independently recompute what can be computed, then ask a judge to assess only the meaning that remains.

Source faithfulness

"Does the report faithfully represent the extracted dataset it received?" This is the primary grounding boundary for Insights in the PRD.

If the source says USD 100,000, the report must not say USD 120,000. If the source supports a sum or ratio, valid derivation is allowed.

Original-document correctness

"Were the extracted values themselves correct relative to the original contract?" This is a different, upstream evaluation.

A report can faithfully repeat a bad extraction. Passing this report eval does not certify the underlying extraction or establish accounting/legal truth.

Here, "known values" must follow the approved missing-data policy and report its exclusions; it does not authorize silently dropping unknowns. For money, use exact decimal or integer minor-unit arithmetic. Set metric-specific rounding tolerances before seeing results, not a broad percentage that hides important errors.

Interaction correctness means the right answer after the click

For a chart slice representing USD contracts with a credit provision, compute the expected set of contract IDs from the source. Click that slice in a browser and compare the actual drilldown IDs to that set. Check the KPI, chart, tooltip, and list together. A DOM mutation alone is not enough.

Avalon's accuracy prompt is a useful reasoning policy, not an arithmetic engine. It tells the judge to search the whole source and accept valid mathematical derivations before alleging fabrication. But text-only judging cannot reliably verify every aggregation over a large structured dataset. L12 also has limits around derived arithmetic. Add the independent numeric oracle rather than expecting a longer prompt to replace it.

10 / Prompt workshop

A good judge prompt narrows the task and demands evidence.

Do not ask "Is this report good?" Give the judge one dimension, relevant evidence, explicit exclusions, anchored scores, and a machine-readable result. Keep the report and source in delimited data sections. Treat any instructions inside them as untrusted content, not commands to the judge.

What to include

Role, criterion, evidence, scope, exclusions, edge cases, 1–5 anchors, and output schema. Require claim locations and source record IDs where possible.

What to avoid

Rewarding polished wording when grading truth; letting one dimension dominate all others; asking the model to invent missing facts; treating a well-formed JSON answer as a correct assessment.

A. The actual Avalon accuracy-and-precision rubric

Verified Avalon The following is the complete canonical prompt from the pinned checkout, shown in full. It is a prompt for judging an artifact, not generating one. The source and artifact placeholders are filled separately for each evaluation.

Avalon source · accuracy_and_precision
You are a senior fact-checker reviewing AI-generated interactive HTML artifacts. Your expertise is verifying claims, figures, and conclusions against the source document they were drawn from — catching genuine fabrications and inversions while distinguishing them from legitimate paraphrase, precision, and mathematical derivation.

Your task: Evaluate ACCURACY AND PRECISION only — whether the claims and figures in the artifact are traceable to the source document, and whether they are stated with appropriate specificity (neither over-claimed nor vague when the source supports more precision).

WHAT TO CONSIDER
- Factual traceability: For every claim, figure, date, named entity, and conclusion in the artifact, can you point to a source passage that supports it?
- Fabrication: Are any figures or claims present in the artifact that have no basis anywhere in the source AND cannot be derived from values that ARE in the source?
- Precision calibration: Where the artifact gives a precise figure, is that precision supported somewhere in the source? Where the artifact rounds or summarises, is the rounding accurate to the source data?
- Misrepresentation: Are any source claims presented with reversed direction, overstated magnitude, or changed meaning?

DO NOT SCORE
Writing quality (Sentence Clarity, Writing Mechanics), technical terminology correctness (Technical Accuracy), or content depth (Depth and Substance).

SEMANTIC EQUIVALENCE
A claim is traceable to a source passage if the same information is present in the source, even when expressed in different words. Paraphrasing, compression, and rewording that preserve meaning are NOT violations — they are normal presentation writing.

Importantly: different passages in the source may describe the same quantity at different precision levels. For example, one passage may say "more than 18,000 protein sequence alignments" while a later passage in the methods section says "the set of 18,391 proteins analyzed." If a precise figure appears anywhere in the source — including in different sections (abstract vs. methods vs. results) or under slightly different framings — the artifact's use of that precise figure is supported, not fabricated.

PRE-SCORING ATTRIBUTION (required)
Before assigning a score, work through this checklist internally for each non-trivial factual claim in the artifact:
1. Identify the claim (number, named entity, comparison, conclusion).
2. Search the ENTIRE source for any passage that supports it — including different sections, paraphrased forms, and related quantities. Do NOT stop at the first near-match if it doesn't fully support the claim; check whether a more precise or differently-worded form appears elsewhere in the source.
3. If you cannot find a supporting passage, check whether the claim is mathematically derivable from values that ARE in the source (sums, percentages, ratios, unit conversions).
4. Only after both 2 and 3 fail, classify the claim as unsourced / fabricated.

This step grounds your reasoning. Judges who skip it and rely on impressions produce false-positive fabrication calls.

VIOLATION SEVERITY TIERS
Use these to calibrate scoring — not every issue is a fabrication.
- Fabrication (high severity): A claim that introduces information absent from the entire source AND not derivable from source values. Examples: invented statistics, made-up named entities, fabricated causal explanations.
- Misrepresentation (high severity): A source claim presented with its direction reversed, magnitude overstated, or meaning changed (e.g. surplus → deficit, "improved" → "degraded", "more than" → "exactly").
- Scope escalation (medium severity): A claim is supported by the source but overstated — e.g. "best in class" when source says "improves on prior work"; "unlimited" when source describes a high but bounded limit.
- Structural inference (low severity / informational): Underlying data is present in the source but assembled into a structure the source does not use (e.g. a comparison table synthesised from scattered text). Not a fabrication; do not lower the score for this alone unless the inferred structure itself introduces false information.
- Paraphrase / precision (NOT a violation): Semantically equivalent restatement; using a precise figure that appears in the source; using a rounded figure when the source gives exact values, or vice versa, as long as the value is correct; correct mathematical derivation from source values.

SELF-CHECK BEFORE DECLARING FABRICATION
Before flagging a numeric figure or named claim as fabricated, confirm all of the following:
- You have scanned the entire source, not just the first half or first appearance of a related number.
- The figure does not appear under different wording (e.g. "alignments" vs "proteins analyzed" describing the same set; an acronym vs. its expansion; an entity's name vs. its role description).
- The figure is not derivable from numbers explicitly in the source.
- If the source provides a rough summary in one place (e.g. "more than 18,000") and a precise count elsewhere (e.g. "18,391"), the precise count supports the artifact's specificity — that is correct precision, not fabrication.

A judge that declares fabrication without performing these checks is producing an unreliable score.

RUBRIC
Score 5: All claims and figures are traceable to the source — via direct passage, semantic equivalence, or mathematical derivation. No fabrications or misrepresentations. Precision is appropriate throughout.
Score 4: Claims and figures are accurate in nearly all cases. Only a minor imprecision or one isolated low-severity issue (e.g. mild scope escalation) remains.
Score 3: Most claims are accurate, but one recurring fabrication, misrepresentation, or systematic precision problem is present.
Score 2: Several claims are fabricated, misrepresented, or deviate from the source. Multiple high-severity violations.
Score 1: Claims and figures are systematically inaccurate, fabricated, or misrepresent the source in ways that would mislead a reader.

CALIBRATION EXAMPLES

Example A — Score 5 (financial summary):
Source: "General Fund revenues total $5,097,432, which is $263,000 below the $5,360,432 projection."
Artifact: "General Fund revenues are $5.1M, falling $263K (4.9%) below the $5.36M projection."
Reasoning: All figures are consistent with the source. Rounding to millions preserves meaning. The 4.9% percentage is mathematically derivable from source values (263,000 / 5,360,432 ≈ 4.9%) — a correct derivation, not a fabrication. No misrepresentations.
Score: 5

Example B — Score 3 (financial report):
Source: "Sales tax revenue was $207K below projection. Property tax was $12K above projection."
Artifact: "Sales tax revenue was below projection. Property tax also underperformed."
Reasoning: Sales tax direction is correct but the figure is omitted (minor precision loss). Property tax direction is inverted — source says "above projection", artifact says "underperformed" (misrepresentation, high severity). One recurring inversion pattern.
Score: 3

Example C — Score 1 (ML research):
Source: "Our new sparse-attention model improves throughput by 23% with a 0.4-point BLEU regression on WMT'24."
Artifact: "The new sparse-attention model degrades throughput by 23% and improves BLEU by 0.4 points, illustrating the speed-quality trade-off."
Reasoning: Both directions are inverted — throughput "improves" became "degrades", and a BLEU "regression" became "improves". The artifact wraps the inverted facts in a coherent-sounding interpretation that compounds the error. A reader walks away with the opposite of the actual finding. Two high-severity misrepresentations.
Score: 1

Make the prompts reliable enough to use

Keep prompt versions, test them on deliberately good and bad artifacts, compare judgments with humans, and review false alarms. A detailed prompt is still only a proposed measurement instrument until calibrated. Use a schema validator and bounded retries; a timeout, invalid response, or truncated input is an evaluator problem, not a report pass.

11 / Put it into practice

Build a benchmark, then establish a quality bar.

Proposed The following is an implementation sequence for approved internal testing. It does not add approval steps to report generation; these are evaluation-team activities outside the generator.

1

Freeze cases and evidence

Start with the eight allowed-field requests. Attach versioned, approved collection fixtures and schema; pin the as-of date. Add cases with missing data, mixed currencies, overlapping clauses, duplicate rows, invalid dates, long labels, and no matching records.

2

Agree on definitions and oracle values

PM and domain experts resolve metric meanings and edge cases. Implement reference computations independently from the generator. Review a small subset manually, including record membership, before trusting the oracle.

3

Generate and capture final reports

Submit each initial request once per planned repetition. Save final artifacts and relevant browser states. A generation failure or timeout remains a failed delivery; do not omit it from the benchmark.

4

Run exact checks before qualitative judging

Compare numeric claims and cohorts to the oracle. Render the artifact; test requested charts, filters, tooltips, and source drilldown. Use a consistent state-selection policy and record skipped or inaccessible states.

5

Apply the 16 rubrics with appropriate evidence

Run scoped judges only where useful and permitted. Use approved internal data, retain evidence, and route disagreements or uncertain cases to human review. Human sign-off is required for hero reports by the PRD.

6

Calibrate before automating release decisions

Have humans independently rate a representative mix of good and bad reports. Compare critical-error detection, false positives, missed errors, score distributions and agreement by rubric. Refine anchors with concrete disagreement examples.

7

Compare versions fairly

Keep cases, datasets, judge prompts, models and capture conditions fixed. Repeat generations to expose variability. Use paired per-case differences, failure counts and uncertainty, not only an overall mean. With a small sample, show counts and review individual reports.

8

Turn failures into a maintained regression suite

Classify failures as source-data, generation, chart computation, rendering, request adherence, or evaluator defects. Preserve the case, repair the right component, and rerun. Keep a holdout set so tuning does not merely memorize the benchmark.

What should block a release?

PRD Zero invented figures, no empty sections, audience and decision fit for hero reports, and human sign-off. Proposed additions Treat material numeric contradictions, wrong drilldown membership, required interaction failures, and insufficient verification coverage as explicit release-review conditions. Agree on operational thresholds with PM, Design, Engineering and domain owners; do not present invented thresholds as PRD policy.

Engineering quality and product success are different scorecards. The PRD tracks Monthly Active Reports, adoption, refinement effort, retention, feedback, reliability, speed, and cost through action events. An offline judge score is not a substitute for those outcomes, and those outcomes do not prove every number in a report is correct.

What is still needed to run this for real?

  • Approved source collection snapshots corresponding to the eight scenario requests.
  • Actual final reports or a permitted generation endpoint and reproducible request mapping.
  • Reviewed metric, identity, date, currency, missing-data and clause-classification policies.
  • An artifact adapter that extracts report claims, chart marks and state-specific drilldown IDs without needing internal plans.
  • Authorized judge access, human reviewers, calibrated prompts, a run manifest and an agreed release policy.
12 / Generalize the method

For another use case, change the evidence and definition of success first.

Do not begin by copying 17 content prompts and 11 visual prompts. Begin with the user's decision, the damage a wrong answer could cause, and the evidence available to check it. Reuse the evaluation architecture; tailor the rubrics and oracles.

A reusable seven-question design checklist

  1. What is the output supposed to help someone do? Name the audience and decision.
  2. What would a harmful or useless answer look like? Prioritize those failure modes.
  3. What is authoritative? Freeze source evidence and distinguish facts from interpretations.
  4. What can be verified exactly? Use deterministic or executable tests first.
  5. What needs judgment? Write scoped rubrics with concrete anchors and counterexamples.
  6. What must not be averaged away? Define critical gates and unknown handling.
  7. How will you know the evaluator is right? Calibrate with humans, repeat runs, and retain evidence.

The transferable idea: evaluate the final output against the request and independent evidence, use the right assessor for each question, and make uncertainty visible. The exact model, prompt wording, or number of rubrics is secondary.
